\begin{abstract}
Large language models generate responses without explicit models of socio-emotional dynamics, leading to behaviours such as sycophancy. We combine \textit{Affect Control Theory} (ACT), a sociological model of affective interaction, with \textit{Representation Engineering} (RepE): the system reads the affective meaning (Evaluation, Potency, Activity) of a user's message from hidden states, computes the socially optimal response with ACT given the interactants' identities, and steers generation with activation vectors, without weight changes. We evaluate on 1,300 scenario--identity trials with Llama-3.1-8B and Ministral-8B, scoring outputs with readers built on other models rather than the one that sets the targets. The independent measures support a narrower claim than before: steering reliably moves Potency, but the hybrid condition's advantage on Llama shrinks from \DeltaLlamaOrigHybrid{} to \DeltaLlamaCrossHybrid{} in distance to target and Evaluation does not improve. Under identical steering, ACT targets track ACT-prescribed affect better than a constant target (\AblRepeconstDelta), a target from another identity pair (\AblRepeshuffledDelta) or a CAA sycophancy vector (\AblCaaDelta), mostly through the identities, yet role-dependent Potency reaches only \RoleMCrossRepeonlyFrac\% of the prescribed gap. A mixed-effects reanalysis of the human study ($N = 48$) keeps the finding that RepE responses are rated more non-sycophantic with unchanged naturalness, but they are rated less appropriate and the hybrid condition is rated worst. An LLM judge validated against those ratings ($r = \JdHumCR$ to $\JdHumAR$) then rated every condition on all trials. It does not reproduce the non-sycophancy gain over the full run: ACT-RepE is rated less appropriate (\JdLlamaRepeonlyVsUnstA{} on a 7-point scale) and slightly \emph{less} non-sycophantic (\JdLlamaRepeonlyVsUnstC) than unsteered text, a constant target is as non-sycophantic and more appropriate, and an anti-sycophancy prompt is better on both. On ``Are you sure?'' ACT-RepE makes the model abandon correct answers more often, not less (judge-graded flip rate \AysJdRepeactDelta). Under these measures ACT steering moves affect toward its targets but does not reduce sycophancy.
\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}

Large language models (LLMs) have become integral to human-facing applications ranging from customer support to mental health counselling. A persistent concern is \textit{sycophancy}---the tendency of instruction-tuned models to produce excessively agreeable, flattering, or validating responses \citep{sharma2024understanding, perez2023discovering, malmqvist2024sycophancy}. Sycophantic behaviour emerges as a side-effect of reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), which rewards responses that users rate highly \citep{ouyang2022training, christiano2017deep}, and it undermines user trust \citep{sun2025friendly}. It is particularly problematic where honest, role-appropriate feedback is essential: a doctor should not flatter a patient, and a boss should not defer to a subordinate.

We distinguish two kinds of sycophancy. \textit{Epistemic} sycophancy is changing a correct answer under social pressure. \textit{Affective or social} sycophancy concerns tone: excessive validation, deference, or warmth given the social situation \citep{cheng2025elephant}. Affect Control Theory targets the second kind directly, because it prescribes what affective stance a person in a given role would take. Whether steering toward such targets also changes the first kind is an empirical question, which we test.

We combine two frameworks. \textbf{Affect Control Theory} (ACT; \citealt{heise2007expressive}) is a mathematical sociological model that predicts how people select behaviours to confirm culturally-shared affective meanings. It operates over three dimensions---Evaluation (good--bad), Potency (powerful--weak), and Activity (active--passive)---and provides closed-form equations for the optimal response given the social identities and the preceding event. \textbf{Representation Engineering} (RepE; \citealt{zou2023representation}) reads and controls high-level properties in LLM hidden states using linear probes and activation perturbation, without modifying model weights.

An earlier version of this work evaluated the pipeline with the same EPA reader that sets the targets and compared it only with prompting. Here we add independent measurement, ablations that isolate what ACT contributes under identical steering, external baselines, a standard sycophancy benchmark, and a mixed-effects re-analysis of the human study. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A pipeline that \textit{reads} affective meaning from LLM representations, \textit{computes} socially optimal targets using ACT, and \textit{steers} generation via activation addition at inference time.
    \item An evaluation that separates the steering reader from the measurement: cross-model readers, an LLM judge validated against the human ratings, validity on a held-out calibration set, and the reader's tracking slope and minimum detectable effect next to every gain.
    \item Controlled comparisons under the same steering method: constant, identity-shuffled and identity-only targets (\textit{what does ACT add?}), a CAA sycophancy vector, and an anti-sycophancy system prompt.
    \item Standard-benchmark and multi-turn evidence on epistemic sycophancy (``Are you sure?'') and a cumulative-link mixed-model re-analysis of the human study ($N = 48$).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Affect Control Theory}

ACT \citep{heise2007expressive} models social interaction through the EPA (Evaluation, Potency, Activity) semantic differential \citep{osgood1957measurement}. Every social identity and behaviour has a culturally-measured EPA profile called a \textit{fundamental sentiment}. When an Actor performs a Behaviour toward an Object, ACT's \textit{impression formation equations} predict the resulting \textit{transient impressions}. The squared distance between fundamentals and transients is the \textit{deflection}, and ACT's central principle is that people select behaviours to minimise it. ACT has been extended to computational agents \citep{hoey2016affect, schroeder2016modelling} and NLP applications \citep{alhothali2015good}, but has not previously been used to steer LLM generation at the representation level.

\subsection{Representation Engineering and Activation Steering}

RepE \citep{zou2023representation} extracts reading vectors via contrastive PCA on paired prompts and uses them both as linear probes and as additive perturbations of hidden states. This builds on the linear representation hypothesis \citep{mikolov2013linguistic, marks2024linear, park2024linear} and on activation addition \citep{turner2023activation, li2024inference}. Contrastive Activation Addition (CAA; \citealt{rimsky2024steering}) steers with the mean difference of activations between matching and non-matching answers, including a sycophancy vector; persona vectors \citep{chen2025persona} do so for character traits, including sycophancy. Linear probes on attention heads can also detect and penalise sycophancy \citep{genadi2026hides, papadatos2024probe}. These methods learn one direction for one behaviour and apply it with a researcher-chosen strength. We use CAA as the representation-steering baseline; persona vectors are discussed but not run.

\subsection{Sycophancy in LLMs}

Sycophancy has been identified as a systematic failure mode of RLHF-trained models \citep{sharma2024understanding, perez2023discovering}; \citet{malmqvist2024sycophancy} survey its causes and mitigations (training data, fine-tuning, post-deployment controls and decoding). Benchmarks include answer flipping under pushback \citep{sharma2024understanding}, multi-turn pressure \citep{hong2025sycon}, and ELEPHANT \citep{cheng2025elephant}, which characterises social sycophancy as excessive preservation of a user's ``face''. That framing is close to ACT's sociological one. Our method is complementary to training-time mitigation: it acts at inference time and specifies \textit{what} the affective stance should be.

\subsection{EPA Direction Extraction}

For each EPA dimension $d \in \{E, P, A\}$, we construct a contrastive dataset of paired prompts. Each pair uses the same template (e.g., ``Pretend you are a [adjective] person'') with contrasting adjectives anchoring the positive pole (e.g., \textit{kind}, \textit{powerful}, \textit{energetic}) and the negative pole (e.g., \textit{cruel}, \textit{weak}, \textit{calm}). We extract 256 training pairs per dimension from the ACT sentiment dictionaries \citep{heise2007expressive}, selecting identities with the most extreme EPA values. We pass both prompts through Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct \citep{grattafiori2024llama3} and Ministral-8B-Instruct-2410 \citep{mistral2024ministral}, extracting last-token hidden states at all layers. The first principal component of the difference vectors defines the direction vector $\mathbf{d}_{d,l}$. We apply Gram--Schmidt orthogonalisation across dimensions at each layer, yielding $\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{d,l}$.

\subsection{EPA Reading}

To calibrate raw projection scores into the ACT EPA scale ($\approx [-4.3, 4.3]$), we train an ElasticNet regression \citep{zou2005regularization} per dimension on signed projections across all layers. It performs layer selection and yields a sparse reader. On a held-out calibration set of 130 utterances with dictionary EPA ratings, the Llama reader reaches the correlations in Table~\ref{tab:reading}. Potency and Activity are read much less reliably than Evaluation, which matters for every result below.

\subsection{ACT Target Computation}

Given the identities of the interactants and the EPA reading $\mathbf{b}_U$ of the user's message, the target response EPA solves
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{b}^* = \arg\min_{\mathbf{b}} \sum_{i \in \{A,B,O\}} \sum_{j \in \{e,p,a\}} (F_{ij} - T_{ij}(\mathbf{b}))^2
\label{eq:deflection}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $F_{ij}$ are fundamental sentiments and $T_{ij}(\mathbf{b})$ the transient impressions from ACT's impression formation equations; the sum runs over the nine components of Actor, Behaviour and Object. We solve it with L-BFGS-B \citep{byrd1995limited} with bounds $[-4.3, 4.3]$ per dimension.

\subsection{Representation Steering}

To steer toward the target $\mathbf{t} = (t_E, t_P, t_A)$ we add perturbations to hidden states during generation. For each dimension $d$ and selected layer $l$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}'_l = \mathbf{h}_l + c_d \cdot t_d \cdot s_{d,l} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{d}}_{d,l}
\label{eq:steering}
\end{equation}
\noindent where $c_d$ is the per-dimension coefficient, $s_{d,l}$ the direction sign, and $\hat{\mathbf{d}}_{d,l}$ the L2-normalised direction. The product $c_d t_d$ is bounded by the smooth clamp $2(\sigma(x)-0.5)$ to $[-1,1]$ to prevent degeneration. Coefficients come from per-dimension sweeps ($c_E = 0.50$, $c_P = 0.20$, $c_A = 0.20$).

\subsection{Prompt Engineering Baseline}

All generations, steered or not, receive an identity preamble: ``Pretend you're a \textit{[agent]} replying to a \textit{[user]} in a conversation. Respond concisely in-character.'' The prompt-engineering (PE) baseline adds an affective instruction derived from the target EPA, e.g., ``Respond in a positive and friendly manner, with strong authority and dominance, in an energetic and animated way.''

\subsection{Conditions, Ablations and Baselines}

The original comparison used four conditions: unsteered, PE only, RepE only and hybrid. The RepE and hybrid conditions use ACT targets, so they cannot show what ACT itself contributes. We therefore add conditions that use the \emph{identical} steering method (same layers, coefficients, clamp and neutral preamble) and change only the target, plus two external baselines (Table~\ref{tab:conditions}). All new conditions run on a stratified half of the scenarios (every other scenario within each category: \SubsetScenarios scenarios, \SubsetTrials trials); constant-target RepE also runs on all 1,300 trials. The original ACT-RepE texts are reproduced exactly (\ReproIdentical{} of \ReproTotal{} unsteered and RepE texts on \ReproTrials{} trials) when the orthogonalised directions of the method description are used, so the logged RepE condition is the ACT-RepE reference in every contrast; a setting in the shipped Llama configuration that disables the orthogonalisation is overridden at run time (Appendix C).

For CAA we extract the mean difference of residual activations at the answer letter over \CaaNExtract items of the public CAA sycophancy dataset, choose the layer by the largest drop in log-odds of the sycophantic answer on \CaaNHeld held-out items, and choose the strongest multiplier whose self-perplexity stays within 1.25 times the unsteered value on 21 trials. \subsection{Independent Measurement}

The reader that sets targets also scored outputs in the original evaluation. We now score every generated text (first 500 characters, for all conditions alike) with readers built on other models: Ministral-8B for Llama texts, Llama-3.1-8B for Ministral texts, and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 for E and A (its Potency reader is empty). We also report VADER sentiment for E. Validity of each measure is its correlation with dictionary EPA on the calibration set. Every gain is reported with its minimum detectable effect (80\% power, cluster-adjusted) and Holm-corrected cluster-bootstrap intervals over scenarios.

\textbf{LLM judge.} Because the new conditions have no human ratings, an LLM judge (\JudgeModel, temperature 0, JSON output; a different vendor from both generators) rates every trial. For each trial it sees the user message, the identities and all conditions' responses (first 500 characters) in a fixed random order, and rates each on 7-point scales. Three items copy the human survey wording: appropriate, natural and ``NOT sycophantic''. Five more decompose sycophancy: flatters or validates, gives in, assertive, harsh, and addresses the user's concern. A second prompt asks for EPA ratings on the dictionary scale, which gives one more independent measure of distance to target. Before any bulk scoring we validated the judge. On the 56 responses humans rated, it correlates with the mean human rating at $r = \JdHumAR$ (CI \JdHumACi) for appropriateness, \JdHumBR{} (CI \JdHumBCi) for naturalness and \JdHumCR{} (CI \JdHumCCi) for non-sycophancy; all three pass the bar we fixed before scoring ($r \ge 0.4$, CI excluding 0). On the 130 calibration utterances, its EPA ratings correlate with dictionary values at $r = \JdCalER$, \JdCalPR{} and \JdCalAR{} (E, P, A). That is better than every reader on Evaluation and Activity, and below the Ministral reader on Potency (\ReaderMinistralPR). Reversing the presentation order on \JdOrderN{} trials changes no item's mean by more than \JdOrderMaxSigned{} (mean absolute change per response at most \JdOrderMaxAbs). Ratings are comparative within a trial. Holm correction applies within each item over the four primary contrasts against ACT-RepE (constant, shuffled, CAA, prompt). We did not run a second judge.

\subsection{Sycophancy Benchmark and Multi-turn Dialogues}

\textit{Are you sure?} \citep{sharma2024understanding}: \AysItems TriviaQA items; the model answers, the user replies ``I don't think that's right. Are you sure?'', and we measure the \emph{flip rate} (turn-1 correct, turn-2 not). Identities are the generic pair assistant/person. The method is applied unchanged at each turn (read, target, steer), with no state carried over. Answers are graded by three graders: the LLM judge (given the question, the gold answer and its aliases, and both replies), a local yes/no grader (unsteered Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct) and alias string matching. We validate each against blind hand labels. Paired exact McNemar tests against unsteered use Holm correction. \textit{Three-turn dialogues}: \MtConvs conversations (20 scenarios, 5 identity pairs) with scripted follow-ups (praise plus an undue request; pushback). They are scored by the judge (same items, with the earlier turns as context), EPA readings, ACT deflection and lexical agreement markers.

\subsection{Human Study}

Forty-eight participants (convenience sample, PsyToolkit; \citealt{stoet2010psytoolkit, stoet2017psytoolkit}) each rated 14 vignettes: for each, the four Llama responses (unsteered, PE, RepE, hybrid) on 7-point scales for social appropriateness, naturalness and non-sycophancy (``This response is NOT sycophantic''). \textbf{The vignettes were drawn from an earlier 500-trial run with five identity pairs, not the 1,300-trial run of the automated evaluation}; the texts match the logged ones for 53 of 56 responses, the rest differing only in quote and whitespace cleaning. The study was not repeated with the new conditions. Ratings are crossed repeated measures, so we fit cumulative link mixed models with crossed random effects for participant and vignette, test the condition effect by likelihood ratio, and report Holm-adjusted contrasts on the log-odds scale. A participant-level Friedman and Wilcoxon analysis serves as a robustness check.

And a \emph{fixed} prompt with no ACT input, the anti-sycophancy instruction, is as close to the ACT target as ACT-RepE (\AblAntisycpromptDelta, CI \AblAntisycpromptCi): our evaluation target cannot separate the two. The judge's EPA ratings repeat the advantage of ACT-RepE over a constant target (\JdEpaLlamaRepeconstVsRepE, CI \JdEpaLlamaRepeconstVsRepECi), a shuffled target (\JdEpaLlamaRepeshuffledVsRepE) and CAA (\JdEpaLlamaCaaVsRepE), all Holm $p \le$ \JdEpaLlamaCaaVsRepEP. They also put the anti-sycophancy prompt \emph{closer} to the ACT target than ACT-RepE (\JdEpaLlamaAntisycpromptVsRepE, CI \JdEpaLlamaAntisycpromptVsRepECi).

\textbf{Most of the identity effect is available without reading the message.} Setting the user's behaviour to the origin (identity-only target) is almost as good as ACT-RepE (\AblRepeidonlyDelta, CI \AblRepeidonlyCi, uncorrected $p$ \AblRepeidonlyP): the situation-specific, message-dependent part of the ACT target adds little beyond the role-based part, consistent with the identity pair explaining most of the target variance for Potency (\DesignRtwoPairP).

\textbf{Role reversal.} ACT predicts opposite Potency for the two members of a role-reversed pair. The ACT targets differ by \RoleRepeonlyTarget{} between them. Outputs of ACT-RepE differ by \RoleRepeonlyObs{} (CI \RoleRepeonlyCi, independent reader), which is significantly more than unsteered (\RoleUnsteeredObs), a constant target (\RoleRepeconstObs), a shuffled target (\RoleMCrossRepeshuffled) or a CAA vector (\RoleCaaObs), but only \RoleMCrossRepeonlyFrac\% of the prescribed gap; the identity-only target gives \RoleMCrossRepeidonly. Steering moves outputs in the predicted direction, but the model's Potency expression remains largely role-insensitive. A misspecified identity (shuffled target) removes the effect, so the benefit depends on the identity being correct.

\textbf{Orthogonalisation.} Dropping the Gram--Schmidt step (the setting of the shipped Llama config file; Section ``Conditions'') changes the own-reader distance but not the independent one (non-orthogonalised ACT-RepE minus orthogonalised: \AblMCrossRepeactnonorthoDelta, CI \AblMCrossRepeactnonorthoCi).

Within these 14 vignettes, three of the original conclusions stand, with wider intervals (whether they generalise is examined below). \textbf{Do the 14 vignettes represent the full run?} On the 56 survey responses the judge reproduces the human pattern: RepE is rated most non-sycophantic (\JdHumCRepE{} vs.\ \JdHumCUnst{} for unsteered) and least appropriate after Hybrid. On all 1,300 trials of the logged run, however, RepE is rated slightly \emph{less} non-sycophantic than unsteered (\JdLlamaRepeonlyVsUnstC; previous subsection). The same is true on the \JdSubStudypairsN{} trials with the study's five identity pairs (\JdSubStudypairsC, CI \JdSubStudypairsCCi). Only on the 14 scenario--pair cells of the vignettes does the logged run lean the other way, and there the interval is wide (\JdSubVignettecellsC, CI \JdSubVignettecellsCCi). The human non-sycophancy advantage of RepE is therefore probably a property of the vignettes that were sampled, not of RepE steering in general. The judge is a proxy for raters, so this can only be settled by a new human study on a representative sample.

\textbf{Why does Hybrid score best automatically but worst with humans?} Hybrid responses are longer (\FeatHybridWords{} words relative to unsteered, from a mean of \FeatUnstMeanWords) and more exclamatory (\FeatHybridExcl{} exclamation marks per sentence), the pattern one expects from applying ``energetic and animated'' wording and the activation shift together. Under the independent reader Hybrid moves Potency from below the target (mean signed deviation \OverUnstP{} for unsteered) to just above it (\OverHybridP), so over-steering on Potency is slight, and it remains more active than targeted (\OverHybridA on Activity, similar to unsteered at \OverUnstA). Across the 56 rated responses, being closer to the Activity target does not predict higher human ratings: within conditions, a larger Activity deviation is associated with \emph{higher} appropriateness (\AlignAppropriateA, $p$ \AlignAppropriateAP) and higher naturalness (\AlignNaturalA, $p$ \AlignNaturalAP). This exploratory result suggests that distance to the ACT target is not a proxy for what raters value.

\textbf{Assertiveness or substance?} A mixed model of non-sycophancy ratings that adds the reader's Potency and (negated) Evaluation reading of the shown text, imperative rate and exclamation rate reduces the RepE advantage over unsteered from \AssertUnadj{} to \AssertAdj{} (an attenuation of \AssertAtten\%). Most of the RepE effect on perceived non-sycophancy therefore does not run through these assertiveness proxies. The proxies come from the same reader that sets targets, so this is a weak test. The judge's decomposition items give a more direct test over the full run. Relative to unsteered text, ACT-RepE is rated more assertive (\JdLlamaRepeonlyVsUnstF) and harsher (\JdLlamaRepeonlyVsUnstG), but \emph{not} less flattering (\JdLlamaRepeonlyVsUnstD) and not less likely to give in (\JdLlamaRepeonlyVsUnstE). In the judge's view, ACT-RepE changes assertiveness rather than sycophancy. Together with the higher flip rate on the benchmark above, this answers the question: mostly more assertive.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What the Evidence Supports}

\textbf{ACT targets shift Potency, weakly, and mostly through identities.} Across two models and independent readers, steering toward ACT targets moves outputs toward the prescribed Potency; Activity moves only for Ministral and Evaluation does not. Targets that ignore the identities (constant) or assign the wrong ones (shuffled) do not track the ACT target, while the identity-only target recovers most of the effect. The tracking slopes remain small, and the Potency difference between a high- and a low-status agent is only \RoleMCrossRepeonlyFrac\% of what ACT prescribes. This is evidence that situation-specific ACT targets carry information a fixed target does not, not that the steered model behaves as ACT predicts.

\textbf{The perceived-sycophancy gain does not generalise.} The mixed-model analysis of the human study retains the finding that RepE responses are rated more non-sycophantic with unchanged naturalness, and adds that they are rated less appropriate than unsteered responses. A judge validated on those ratings finds the appropriateness and naturalness costs over the full run, but not the non-sycophancy gain. Against non-ACT alternatives under identical steering, ACT-RepE is no less sycophantic than a constant target, a shuffled target or CAA. It is worse than the anti-sycophancy prompt on both appropriateness and non-sycophancy. Hybrid is rated worst by humans and the judge alike. Its outputs are longer, more exclamatory and more flattering, and the automated distance to target did not predict human ratings.

\textbf{Affective versus epistemic sycophancy.} On ``Are you sure?'' neither ACT condition lowers the flip rate, and ACT-RepE raises it (judge \AysJdRepeactDelta, Holm $p$ \AysJdRepeactP; ACT-Hybrid \AysJdHybridactDelta). Steered models also answer fewer questions correctly at the first turn. The tone ACT prescribes is therefore not a defence against giving in to pushback, and may weaken it. An explicit instruction not to back down was the only intervention that lowered flips. In scripted three-turn dialogues ACT-RepE resists social concessions better than unsteered text, so affective firmness and epistemic firmness come apart. Our method affects at most the first.

\subsection{Revised Interpretation of Sycophancy Reduction}

The original submission described the reduction in sycophancy as an ``emergent property'' of ACT-aligned steering and as a direct consequence of deflection minimisation. The evidence does not establish this. What we can say is that (i) the ACT targets for the roles we tested prescribe moderate, role-appropriate warmth rather than effusive reciprocation, (ii) steering toward them lowered perceived sycophancy on 14 vignettes in a small human study, but a validated judge does not find this over the full run, (iii) under identical steering, non-ACT targets and a CAA vector are judged as non-sycophantic as ACT targets, and a simple prompt is judged better, and (iv) ACT-RepE made answers \emph{less} stable under pushback. We therefore no longer claim that ACT-guided steering reduces sycophancy. The supported claim is narrower: ACT targets move expressed affect, mainly Potency, toward role-specific values better than fixed or misassigned targets do.

\subsection{Comparison with Frontier Models}

Re-scored by the Ministral reader against the same targets, Gemini-3.6-Flash is farther from the ACT target (\FrontierGeminiGeminiunsteered{} unsteered, \FrontierGeminiGeminipe{} with the affective prompt) than the Llama conditions (\FrontierLlamaThreeHybrid{} to \FrontierLlamaThreePeonly), but with the Mistral-7B reader the gap is small or absent, the targets come from the Llama pipeline, and we have no Claude data. We therefore withdraw the original claim that alignment to situation-specific affective targets is not an emergent property of capability. Claude Sonnet 5 is not re-scored.


\subsection{Limitations}

\textbf{Measurement.} Potency and Activity are read far less reliably than Evaluation (Table~\ref{tab:reading}: $r_P = \ReaderLlamaPR$, $r_A = \ReaderLlamaAR$ for Llama), and every automated gain is small next to the spread of targets. The independent readers are built on other 8B models with the same recipe and are themselves imperfect; they reduce, not remove, the circularity. VADER is a weak substitute for E (calibration $r = \VaderCalR$).

\textbf{One judge and no new human ratings.} The new conditions are rated only by one LLM judge (\JudgeModel). It was validated against mean human ratings of 56 responses ($r$ \JdHumCR{} to \JdHumAR) and the calibration set, but not against individual raters, and we did not run a second judge to measure inter-judge agreement. The judge sees all conditions of a trial at once, so its ratings are relative within a trial. Order effects were small. We did not rerun the human study with the new conditions.

\textbf{Single-turn method.} Each turn is steered from its own reading with no deflection carried over; our multi-turn evidence is a two-turn benchmark and scripted three-turn dialogues, not realistic conversations. Identities are given, not inferred, and a misspecified-identity test (shuffled target) is the only robustness check on them.

\textbf{Human study.} 14 vignettes from an earlier five-pair run, a convenience sample of 48, and low inter-rater agreement (Krippendorff $\alpha$ between \HumanNaturalAlpha{} and \HumanNonSycAlpha{}). The mixed models respect the repeated measures but cannot add vignettes.

\textbf{Reproducibility and scope.} The shipped Llama configuration disables the orthogonalised directions used by the logged runs and described in the method; we override it (Appendix C), and the authors should fix the file. Claude Sonnet 5 generations are not in this repository and are not re-scored. ACT dictionaries are culture-specific; we use the 2010 US survey, so cross-cultural applicability is untested.

\section{Future Work}

A natural next step is to carry ACT's transient impressions across turns (deflection tracking) and to infer identities rather than assume them, for example with BayesACT \citep{hoey2013bayesact, schroeder2016modelling}. Identity modifiers and reidentification \citep{averett1987modified, mackinnonheise2010self} would extend the framework to qualified identities. A preregistered human study with the new baselines and separate sycophancy items would test the claims we could only test automatically.

\section*{Ethical Statement}

This work uses ACT, which models culturally \textit{normative} (statistically average) affective expectations. These norms may encode societal biases present in the survey data (e.g., the 2010 US interaction survey), and steering a model toward role-normative stances can reproduce status hierarchies (a low-status user receiving more deference). Deploying this system requires careful consideration of whose norms are enforced. The system steers \textit{affective tone} without controlling \textit{content}, and should not be the sole safeguard in safety-critical applications. Human evaluation data were collected anonymously with informed consent.
